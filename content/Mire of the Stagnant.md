#plane 
Written by: Osric the Vedalken
## The Mire of the Stagnant
> _“Nothing here is forbidden. Nothing here is required. That is precisely the problem.”_

Of all the Branches I have visited, the Mire of the Stagnant is perhaps the most deceptively peaceful.

There are no screaming crowds.

No endless wars.

No elaborate tortures.

No great beasts hunting the damned through the darkness.

There is only mud.

And fog.

And the overwhelming sensation that whatever it is you were supposed to be doing can wait until tomorrow.

Tomorrow is, in fact, very popular here.

I have met souls who have been waiting for tomorrow for centuries.

Sloth is often misunderstood as simple laziness. This is an incomplete definition. Laziness is merely one expression of it.

Sloth is surrender.

It is the abandonment of effort, direction, responsibility, and ultimately the self.

The physically slothful refuse to move.

The mentally slothful refuse to think.

The emotionally slothful refuse to feel deeply enough to act.

And eventually, all three become the same thing.

A person stops deciding where they are going.

They simply remain where they are.

And if someone else comes along and decides for them?

So much the better.

---

## First Impressions
The Mire of the Stagnant is an immense wetland extending farther than I have yet been able to map.

I say _wetland_ for the sake of academic convenience.

Calling it a swamp implies that something happens within it.

Nothing happens here.

Black water stretches between enormous trees whose roots disappear beneath the surface. Thick grey fog hangs permanently above the ground, occasionally parting enough to reveal distant islands of moss, ruined buildings, half-sunken roads, or the remains of structures whose inhabitants apparently abandoned them halfway through construction.

The trees do not sway.

There is no wind.

The water barely moves.

Even the rain seems reluctant to fall.

Everything is coated in a thick layer of pale resin. It drips slowly from branches, gathers on stones, and forms translucent curtains between trees.

I initially believed it to be sap.

I was later informed that it is condensed "_potential_".

I asked what that meant.

My guide shrugged.

I decided not to ask again.

Walking through the Mire is exhausting.

Not because the terrain is particularly difficult, but because every movement feels unnecessary.

Lifting one's foot becomes an argument with oneself.

Taking another step requires conscious effort.

Reaching for one's satchel feels like an unreasonable demand.

After approximately three hours of travel, I found myself sitting against a tree and thinking:

_Perhaps I should simply remain here for a little while._

I remained there for six hours.

I am recording this because it is important research.

## The Nature of Sloth
Like the other branches, the Mire does not create something entirely foreign within its inhabitants.

It amplifies what is already there.

But sloth does something particularly insidious.

It makes inaction feel reasonable.

A tired traveler does not suddenly become incapable of movement.

They simply think:

_I can rest first._

A frightened person does not suddenly lose all courage.

They think:

_Someone else will handle it._

A person uncertain about their future does not lose the ability to choose.

They think:

_I'll decide later._

And later becomes tomorrow.

Tomorrow becomes next week.

Next week becomes next year.

Eventually, a life can pass without a single decision ever truly belonging to the person who lived it.

This is the true horror of the Mire.

It does not force its inhabitants to remain.

It convinces them that remaining is easier.

---

# The Three Faces of Sloth
The scholars of [[Materia]] generally divide Sloth into three expressions.

I believe the Mire itself recognizes them.

I have begun referring to them as the Body, the Mind, and the Heart.

My guide corrected me and told me the locals call them the Weight, the Fog, and the Yielding.

I prefer the academic terminology.

Mostly because the local terminology sounds better, and I wish the idea was mine first so that I could have coined it.

---

## The Weight — Physical Sloth
The most obvious form.

The body becomes heavy.

Movement requires effort far beyond what it should.

Muscles weaken without atrophy.

Limbs feel as though they are filled with stones.

The longer one remains still, the harder movement becomes.

The inhabitants often lie partially submerged in the mire, sometimes for decades.

Some sleep.

Others are awake.

Many simply stare into the fog.

There are enormous beds scattered throughout certain regions of the swamp. Some are built into trees. Others float upon the water.

I was told they are not prisons.

Anyone can leave.

I watched a man spend three days attempting to sit upright.

On the third day, he stopped halfway.

He looked at me.

I asked whether he needed assistance.

He said, “Not particularly.”

He then lay back down.

I asked when he intended to try again.

“Eventually.”

He did not sound concerned.

I suspect this is the most common answer in the Mire.

---

## The Fog — Mental Sloth
Mental Sloth is considerably more dangerous.

Here, thought itself becomes labor.

Questions become tiring.

Learning becomes tedious.

Curiosity becomes irritating.

Decision-making becomes exhausting.

The fog of the Mire does more than obscure one's surroundings.

A person may look toward a distant road and know perfectly well that it leads somewhere.

They simply cannot bring themselves to wonder where.

A scholar may encounter a book containing knowledge they desperately sought during life.

Opening it feels like work.

A leader may be presented with a crisis requiring immediate action.

They sit.

They consider.

They decide someone else will probably know what to do.

The most disturbing inhabitants are those who have surrendered their capacity to choose entirely.

They wait for instructions.

Then they follow them.

Not because they are compelled.

Because deciding otherwise would require effort.

I spoke to one soul who had apparently served six different masters since arriving in the Mire.

I asked why.

He looked genuinely confused.

“They told me where to go.”

“Did you agree with them?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you disagree?”

“I don't know.”

“Then why did you follow them?”

He thought about this for nearly a minute.

“I suppose they had already decided.”

That answer stayed with me.

---

## The Yielding — Emotional Sloth
This is perhaps the most difficult form to recognize.

Emotional Sloth is not the absence of emotion.

It is more so the refusal to engage with emotion.

The grieving refuse to grieve.

The angry refuse to confront their anger.

The lonely refuse to seek companionship.

The guilty refuse to apologize.

The loving refuse to admit that they love.

The miserable refuse to ask whether they could be happier.

Every difficult emotion becomes something to be postponed.

Eventually, the soul becomes accustomed to letting other people determine what it should feel.

One's family says, _You should be happy._

So one is happy.

A lover says, _You should stay._

So one stays.

A superior says, _This is what you are meant to do._

So one does it.

A tyrant says, _You belong to me._

And the soul, exhausted from deciding otherwise, simply answers:

_All right._

This, I believe, is where sloth becomes something much darker than laziness.

The ultimate surrender was never surrendering one's body.

It is surrendering authorship of oneself.

---

# The Roads That Go Nowhere
There are roads throughout the Mire.

They are among the strangest features of the Branch.

Some are ancient stone roads.

Others are wooden bridges.

Some appear to have been constructed from furniture, doors, bookshelves, or even the bones of enormous creatures.

All of them lead somewhere.

None of them seem to matter.

Travelers can walk for days and eventually arrive somewhere they could have reached by taking another road.

Others simply curve back toward where they began.

The locals call these **The Roads of Tomorrow**.

I asked why.

One of them answered:

“Because tomorrow we will take the right one.”

I asked which road that was.

He pointed toward the fog.

I asked whether we should go there.

He said:

“Tomorrow.”

---

# The Houses of Others
Perhaps the most unsettling structures in the Mire are the houses.

They are warm.

Dry.

Comfortable.

Some are furnished exactly as they were during their inhabitants' mortal lives.

Others belong to people the inhabitants never met.

Inside, there is always food.

A bed.

A fire.

Someone willing to tell you what to do.

This is the great temptation of the Mire.

You do not have to decide.

Someone else will decide for you.

Someone else will cook.

Someone else will work.

Someone else will speak.

Someone else will choose where you sleep.

Someone else will determine what you are worth.

There is always a chair waiting.

There is always someone willing to tell you to sit down.

And there is always another chair after that.

---

# The Branded
The branded of the Mire are unusually difficult to distinguish from one another.

Many have allowed their bodies to soften into the swamp. Limbs trail behind them like roots. Some have become permanently attached to beds, chairs, trees, or the foundations of buildings.

Others remain physically intact but seem perpetually exhausted.

Their greatest distinction, however, is not physical.

It is that many no longer describe their lives using active language.

They do not say:

_I chose._

They say:

_It happened._

They do not say:

_I stayed._

They say:

_There was nowhere else to go._

They do not say:

_I obeyed._

They say:

_That was what I was told to do._

I asked one branded whether he regretted something from his mortal life.

He stared into the water for a long time.

Eventually he answered:

“I suppose I should.”

That may be the saddest answer I have recorded in [[Nehvarra]] so far.

---

# The Demons of Sloth
The native demons of the Mire are strange.

They do not hunt.

They rarely attack.

Many will simply sit beside travelers for hours.

Some resemble enormous insects with heavy, drooping bodies. Others are pale, bloated creatures whose limbs have become little more than roots. Some appear as faceless attendants carrying pillows, blankets, food, and other comforts.

Their primary instinct is to encourage.

“Rest.”

“There's no hurry.”

“You can do it tomorrow.”

“Someone else will handle it.”

“You've done enough.”

“Why bother?”

They are remarkably polite.

I have come to distrust them immensely.

---

# Dremotha, Queen of Stagnation
I expected the ruler of sloth to be asleep.

She was not.

[[Dremotha]] was awake.

She was simply doing nothing.

She is enormous.

Calling her a giantess is technically accurate but fails to convey the scale. Her lower body disappears into the Mire itself, her flesh and the swamp apparently having become indistinguishable long ago.

Trees grow from her back.

Moss covers her shoulders.

Her hair hangs in thick, wet strands that disappear into the water.

Her eyes are half-closed.

Her breathing is so slow that I initially mistook her for part of the landscape.

When I finally found her, I asked:

“What is sloth?”

Dremotha took nearly three minutes to answer.

“Why d'ya need ta know?”

I explained that I was conducting research.

“Then ya've done too much.”

I admit I disliked her immediately.

I asked whether sloth was laziness.

“Nahh.”

Indifference?

“Nahhh.”

Rest?

“Nahhhh.”

Then what?

Dremotha opened one eye.

“Sloth is 'ciding ya no need ta decide.”

The swamp became completely silent.

I asked her what she meant.

She closed her eye again.

“Ya stop movin'. Then ya stop thinkin'. Then ya stop feelin'.”

Her voice was barely louder than the rain.

“An' 'ventually, 'omeone else moves for ya.”

I asked whether that was the purpose of her Branch.

She smiled.

“Nahhhhh.”

A pause.

“It's tha reward.”

I wrote that sentence down.

Then I left.

It took me two hours to stand up.

---

# The Throne of Stagnation
Dremotha's domain is not a palace.

She has no need for one.

Her throne is the Mire itself.

The deeper one travels into the branch, the less distinguishable the landscape becomes from Dremotha's body. Roots pulse beneath the water like veins. Pools breathe. Trees bend toward sounds that no living creature can hear.

At the center lies a vast expanse of perfectly still water.

Nothing grows there.

Nothing dies there.

Nothing moves.

Dremotha rests beneath its surface.

The Branded call it **The Stillness**.

They say that if you remain in the water long enough, you eventually stop remembering why you wanted to leave.

I have no intention of testing this.

I have, however, written down the location.

For academic purposes.

Obviously.

---

# The Philosophy of the Mire
Every branch has a truth hidden beneath its tortured existence.

The Mire's is particularly simple.

A life does not need to be destroyed to be wasted.

A person does not need to suffer.

They do not need to fail spectacularly.

They need only stop choosing.

The Mire does not punish its inhabitants by taking away their freedom.

It gives them freedom without motivation.

It gives them infinite time without purpose.

It gives them rest without recovery.

It gives them comfort without fulfillment.

And, perhaps worst of all, it gives them other people willing to decide their lives for them.

The physical body becomes still.

The mind becomes quiet.

The heart becomes passive.

Eventually, the person becomes little more than a passenger within their own existence.

That is sloth.

Not merely refusing to work.

Not merely sleeping.

Not merely being tired.

It is surrendering oneself to inertia until there is no longer enough of oneself left to resist it.

The Mire does not ask:

_Why did you stop?_

It asks something far more dangerous.

_Why start again?_

And after enough time here, most souls can no longer remember the answer.
